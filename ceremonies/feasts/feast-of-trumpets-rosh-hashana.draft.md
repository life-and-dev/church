# The Feast of Trumpets: A Day of Remembrance and Anticipation

> In the seventh month, on the first day of the month, you shall observe a day of solemn rest, a memorial proclaimed with blast of trumpets, a holy convocation. You shall not do any ordinary work, and you shall present a food offering to the LORD. — Leviticus 23:24-25 (ESV)

The Feast of Trumpets, often called Rosh Hashanah in modern Jewish tradition, is a time that invites deep reflection and expectant joy. This appointed time occurs on the first day of the seventh month of the biblical calendar. While many recognize it as the Jewish New Year, the scriptures describe it as a day of solemn rest and a memorial proclaimed with the sounding of trumpets. This article explores the origins of this festival, its historical observance, and its prophetic connection to the return of the Messiah.

## Biblical Origins and Meaning

The Hebrew name for this festival is Yom Teruah, which translates to "Day of Shouting" or "Day of Blowing." It is one of the seven feasts of the LORD established in the Torah. Unlike other festivals that commemorate specific historical events like the Exodus, the Feast of Trumpets is primarily defined by the blast of the shofar, a trumpet made from a ram's horn.

"Rosh Hashanah" means "head of the year." This designation reflects a later rabbinic tradition that marks the beginning of the civil year in the month of Tishrei (pronounced "Tish-ray"). [It is a time of celebration of the new year](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/rosh-hashanah-101/). However, the biblical religious year actually begins in the spring with the month of Nisan. Tishrei is the seventh month, and its first day serves as a wake-up call for the upcoming High Holy Days, including the Day of Atonement.

> On the first day of the seventh month you shall have a holy convocation. You shall not do any ordinary work. It is a day for you to blow the trumpets. — Numbers 29:1 (ESV)

## Historical Observance and Restoration

Throughout Israel's history, the observance of these memorial days occasionally fell into disuse. A notable restoration occurred during the time of Nehemiah and Ezra. After the exiles returned to Jerusalem and rebuilt the walls, the people gathered on the first day of the seventh month at the Water Gate.

As Ezra the scribe read the Torah, the people wept because they realized how long they had lived in ignorance of God's requirements. Nehemiah and the Levites encouraged the people to move from mourning to celebration, as the day was holy to the LORD. [Jews for Jesus notes](https://jewsforjesus.org/jewish-resources/jewish-holidays/rosh-hashanah/) that the sound of the trumpets reminded the people of God's faithfulness and hinted at the future coming of the Messiah.

> Go your way. Eat the fat and drink sweet wine and send portions to anyone who has nothing ready, for this day is holy to our LORD. And do not be grieved, for the joy of the LORD is your strength. — Nehemiah 8:10 (ESV)

> So the Levites calmed all the people, saying, "Be quiet, for this day is holy; do not be grieved." — Nehemiah 8:11 (ESV)

The celebration involved sharing food with those in need and rejoicing in the understanding of God's Word. The sound of the shofar served as a call to assemble, an alarm for spiritual vigilance, and a reminder of the covenant made at Mount Sinai.

## Prophetic Significance and Fulfillment

In messianic thought, the spring feasts were fulfilled by the first coming of Jesus. Passover pointed to His sacrifice, and First Fruits to His resurrection. The fall feasts, beginning with the Feast of Trumpets, are widely seen as foreshadowing events surrounding His second coming.

The sounding of the trumpet is a recurring theme in the New Testament regarding the return of Christ. It serves as a signal for the gathering of the elect and the resurrection of the dead.

> Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you. — Ephesians 5:14 (ESV)

> For the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a cry of command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the sound of the trumpet of God. And the dead in Christ will rise first. — 1 Thessalonians 4:16 (ESV)

> And he will send out his angels with a loud trumpet call, and they will gather his elect from the four winds, from one end of heaven to the other. — Matthew 24:31 (ESV)

The blast of the shofar at the Feast of Trumpets reminds believers to remain spiritually vigilant. It expresses a yearning for the appearance of the Messiah and the ultimate restoration of all things.

## Common Critiques and Defense

Some argue that observing biblical feasts is unnecessary for modern believers, citing passages like Colossians 2.

> Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ. — Colossians 2:16-17 (ESV)

Critiques often suggest that because these festivals are "shadows," they no longer hold value once the "substance" (Christ) has arrived. However, a common defense is that shadows provide a precise outline of the substance they represent. Just as the spring feasts provided a pattern for the Messiah's first coming, the fall feasts offer a prophetic roadmap for His return. Paul's instruction was not to abolish the feasts, but to ensure that no one is judged for how they observe them as they point toward Christ.

## Modern Observance

Today, many believers continue to observe the Feast of Trumpets as a way to honor biblical tradition and look forward to the future.

* The sounding of the shofar remains the central feature, acting as a reminder of the Messiah's return.
* Families celebrate with festive meals, often including sweet foods like apples and honey to symbolize a "sweet year."
* Believers practice generosity by sharing food and resources with those who have less.
* The day is kept as a time of rest from ordinary work, allowing for focus on spiritual reflection and community gathering.
