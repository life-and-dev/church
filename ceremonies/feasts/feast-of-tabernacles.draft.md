# The Feast of Tabernacles

The Feast of Tabernacles, also known as the Festival of Shelters or Sukkot, is
one of the most joyous celebrations in the biblical calendar. While many
associate it with ancient Israel's wilderness journey, its themes of divine
presence and future hope resonate throughout the entire biblical narrative. This
article explores the significance of this feast, its observance in the New
Testament, and its profound prophetic implications for the future.

## Observance of Feasts in the New Testament

The question of whether biblical feasts remain applicable is a subject of
discussion among believers. Several passages in the New Testament indicate that
early followers of Christ continued to observe these appointed times.

The Apostle Paul demonstrated a commitment to the biblical calendar. He hastened
to be in Jerusalem for the day of Pentecost (Acts 20:16). He also intended to
stay in Ephesus until Pentecost (1 Corinthians 16:8). Furthermore, believers are
encouraged to keep the Feast of Unleavened Bread in a spiritual sense.

> For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed. 8 Let us therefore
> celebrate the festival, not with the old leaven, the leaven of malice and
> evil, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth. — 1 Corinthians
> 5:7-8 (ESV)

However, some passages are often cited to argue against the necessity of these
observances.

> But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of
> woman, born under the law, 5 to redeem those who were under the law, so that
> we might receive adoption as sons. — Galatians 4:4-5 (ESV)
>
> But now that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by God, how can
> you turn back again to the weak and worthless elementary principles of the
> world, whose slaves you want to be once more? 10 You observe days and months
> and seasons and years! — Galatians 4:8-10 (ESV)
>
> And you, who were dead in your trespasses and the uncircumcision of your
> flesh, God made alive together with him, having forgiven us all our
> trespasses, 14 by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its
> legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross. 15 He disarmed the
> rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in
> him. 16 Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and
> drink, or with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. 17 These are
> a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ. —
> Colossians 2:13-17 (ESV)

The ordinances, or specific ways the feasts are observed, are regarded as a
shadow of a greater reality found in Christ. Observing these feasts without a
focus on Christ is often compared to a form of enslavement. However, keeping a
feast to honor Christ is believed to lead to a blessing and entry into the
presence of the LORD. This remains true even if the technical details of the
observance are not perfect. Supporting scriptures include Revelation 3:20 and
Isaiah 56. Mark 2:23-28 also identifies Jesus as the Lord of the Sabbath.

### Defending the Relevance of the Feasts

Critics often argue that passages like Colossians 2:16-17 abolish the need for
observing feasts. They suggest that because Christ is the "substance," the
"shadows" are no longer necessary. However, a different perspective suggests
that Paul was addressing the manner of observance rather than the validity of
the feasts themselves. In this view, the Colossian believers were being judged
by ascetics for the festive way they celebrated. The use of the present tense in
"are a shadow of things to come" indicates that these festivals still point
toward future events in God's plan.

## Scriptural Mandate for the Feast of Tabernacles

This festival begins on the 15th day of the 7th month. During the period of
service in the tent of meeting, the priests received specific instructions
regarding their conduct.

> Drink no wine or strong drink, you or your sons with you, when you go into
> the tent of meeting, lest you die. It shall be a statute forever throughout
> your generations. — Leviticus 10:9 (ESV)

The primary mandate for the feast is found in the book of Leviticus.

> And the LORD spoke to Moses, saying, 34 “Speak to the people of Israel,
> saying, On the fifteenth day of this seventh month and for seven days is the
> Feast of Booths to the LORD. 35 On the first day shall be a holy convocation;
> you shall not do any ordinary work. 36 For seven days you shall present food
> offerings to the LORD. On the eighth day you shall hold a holy convocation
> and present a food offering to the LORD. It is a solemn assembly; you shall
> not do any ordinary work. ... 39 “On the fifteenth day of the seventh month,
> when you have gathered in the produce of the land, you shall celebrate the
> feast of the LORD seven days. On the first day shall be a solemn rest, and on
> the eighth day shall be a solemn rest. 40 And you shall take on the first day
> the fruit of splendid trees, branches of palm trees and boughs of leafy trees
> and willows of the brook, and you shall rejoice before the LORD your God
> seven days. 41 You shall celebrate it as a feast to the LORD for seven days
> in the year. It is a statute forever throughout your generations; you shall
> celebrate it in the seventh month. 42 You shall dwell in booths for seven
> days. All native Israelites shall dwell in booths, 43 that your generations
> may know that I made the people of Israel dwell in booths when I brought them
> out of the land of Egypt: I am the LORD your God.” — Leviticus 23:33-36,39-43
> (ESV)

The observance of this festival continued during the time of the kings. King
Solomon sacrificed offerings to the LORD according to the requirement for the
three annual festivals (2 Chronicles 8:12-13). The men of Israel assembled
before the king at this festival, which occurred in early autumn (1 Kings 8:2).
The prophet Ezekiel also recorded instructions for observing this feast in a
future context (Ezekiel 45:25).

## Jesus Christ and the Feast of Tabernacles

Jesus Christ observed the Feast of Tabernacles and used its traditions to reveal
his identity and mission.

> Now the Jews' Feast of Booths was at hand. — John 7:2 (ESV)

During this festival, several ceremonies took place, including a water libation
rite where water was poured at the altar. This ceremony [reminded the people of
the water provided from the rock](https://hermeneutics.stackexchange.com/questions/96364/why-did-jesus-announce-john-737-on-the-last-day-of-the-feast-of-tabernacles)
during the wilderness journey. On the final day of the feast, Jesus stood up and
made a significant declaration.

> On the last day of the feast, the great day, Jesus stood up and cried out,
> “If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink. 38 Whoever believes in me,
> as the Scripture has said, ‘Out of his heart will flow rivers of living
> water.’” 39 Now this he said about the Spirit, whom those who believed in him
> were to receive, for as yet the Spirit had not been given, because Jesus was
> not yet glorified. — John 7:37-39 (ESV)

## Future Fulfillment of the Feast of Tabernacles

The Feast of Tabernacles also points toward a future time when God will dwell
permanently with his people. The prophet Zechariah speaks of a time when all
nations will come to Jerusalem to keep this feast.

> Then everyone who survives of all the nations that have come against
> Jerusalem shall go up year after year to worship the King, the LORD of hosts,
> and to keep the Feast of Booths. — Zechariah 14:16 (ESV)

This future reality is also echoed in the book of Revelation, where the ultimate
fulfillment of God "tabernacling" with humanity is described.

> And I heard a loud voice from the throne saying, “Behold, the dwelling place
> of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and
> God himself will be with them as their God. — Revelation 21:3 (ESV)

## Conclusion

The Feast of Tabernacles serves as a powerful reminder of God's past provision,
his present invitation through Christ, and the future hope of his eternal
dwelling with humanity. It remains a significant [observance in the New
Testament](#observance-of-feasts-in-the-new-testament), grounded in a clear
[scriptural mandate](#scriptural-mandate-for-the-feast-of-tabernacles).
Ultimately, the feast finds its deepest meaning in the [identity and mission of
Jesus Christ](#jesus-christ-and-the-feast-of-tabernacles) and its [future
fulfillment](#future-fulfillment-of-the-feast-of-tabernacles) in the kingdom of
God.
