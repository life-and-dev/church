# The Feast of the Passover

The Passover, known as Pesach in Hebrew, stands as one of the most significant
observances in the biblical calendar. It commemorates the deliverance of the
Israelites from slavery in Egypt and serves as a foundational event for both
Jewish tradition and Christian theology. By exploring the specific requirements
and historical context of this feast, one can gain a deeper understanding of its
enduring impact on faith and worship.

## Biblical Institution

The Passover was instituted by the LORD as a perpetual memorial of the night He
brought the Israelites out of Egypt.

> It was a night of watching by the LORD, to bring them out of the land of
> Egypt; so this same night is a night of watching kept to the LORD by all the
> people of Israel throughout their generations. — Exodus 12:42 (ESV)

The observance begins on the fourteenth day of the first month.

> On the fourteenth day of the first month is the LORD's Passover, — Numbers
> 28:16 (ESV)

## Regulations and Observance

Specific instructions were given regarding who could participate and how the
meal was to be consumed. Participants were required to be circumcised to ensure
that the covenant community shared in the meal.

> This is the statute of the Passover: no foreigner shall eat of it, but every
> slave that is bought for money may eat of it after you have circumcised him. —
> Exodus 12:43-44 (ESV)

The meal was to be eaten within a single house. None of the meat was to be taken
outside. Additionally, a strict requirement was made regarding the preparation
of the lamb.

> It shall be eaten in one house; you shall not take any of the flesh outside
> the house, and you shall not break any of its bones. — Exodus 12:46 (ESV)

## Connection to Unleavened Bread

While the Passover sacrifice occurs on the fourteenth day, it is closely linked
to the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread which begins on the fifteenth day.

> And on the fifteenth day of this month is a feast. Seven days shall unleavened
> bread be eaten. On the first day there shall be a holy convocation. You shall
> not do any ordinary work. — Numbers 28:17-18 (ESV)

Historical records and scripture indicate that the Passover meal is often eaten
as the first day of Unleavened Bread commences. In common biblical terminology,
the terms are sometimes used interchangeably to describe the entire festival
period.

> Now the Feast of Unleavened Bread drew near, which is called the Passover. —
> Luke 22:1 (ESV)

## New Testament Fulfillment

For Christians, the Passover finds its ultimate fulfillment in the person and
sacrifice of Jesus Christ. He is often identified as the Lamb of God who takes
away the sin of the world.

> Cleanse out the old leaven that you may be a new lump, as you really are
> unleavened. For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed. — 1
> Corinthians 5:7 (ESV)

The requirement that no bones of the Passover lamb be broken was notably
fulfilled during the crucifixion of Jesus.

> For these things took place that the Scripture might be fulfilled: “Not one of
> his bones will be broken.” — John 19:36 (ESV)

## Perspective on Continued Observance

There are varying views on whether Christians should continue to observe the
Passover. Some argue that since Christ fulfilled the law, the physical
observance is no longer necessary. They often cite passages such as Colossians
2:16-17.

> Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or
> with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of
> the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ. — Colossians 2:16-17
> (ESV)

Others suggest that Paul was defending the believers' right to observe these
days against critics who imposed extra-biblical asceticism. From this
perspective, the shadows are not obsolete. They rather point toward the ongoing
reality of Christ. This makes the celebration a meaningful way to honor the
Savior.

## Conclusion

The Passover serves as a powerful reminder of God's deliverance and the
sacrifice of Jesus Christ. It remains a central theme in biblical theology. It
links the [deliverance from Egypt](#biblical-institution) with the [redemption
found in Christ](#new-testament-fulfillment). Whether viewed as a historical
memorial or a continued [spiritual practice](#perspective-on-continued-observance),
the feast highlights the theme of [divine grace and covenant faithfulness](#regulations-and-observance)
throughout the generations.
