# The Feast of Weeks: Shavuot and Pentecost

The Feast of Weeks, known in Hebrew as Shavuot and in Greek as Pentecost, marks
a significant moment in the biblical calendar. This festival connects the
harvest cycle to monumental spiritual events, from the giving of the Law at
Sinai to the outpouring of the Holy Spirit in Jerusalem. Many wonder if these
ancient observances still hold meaning for believers today. By examining the
scriptures and the life of the early church, one can discover the deep
connections between these harvests and the work of Christ.

## Apostolic Observance of the Feasts

Evidence in the New Testament suggests that the apostles and the early church
continued to recognize the biblical festivals. The apostle Paul, for instance,
made efforts to be present in Jerusalem for specific feasts.

> For Paul had decided to sail past Ephesus, so that he might not have to spend
> time in Asia, for he was hastening to be at Jerusalem, if possible, on the day
> of Pentecost. — Acts 20:16 (ESV)

In his letters, Paul also mentioned his plans to remain in certain locations
until the conclusion of these festivals.

> But I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost. — 1 Corinthians 16:8 (ESV)

Beyond mere scheduling, Paul used the festivals to explain the work of Christ to
the believers. He encouraged the church in Corinth to observe the Feast of
Unleavened Bread in a spiritual sense, centered on the sacrifice of Jesus.

> Cleanse out the old leaven that you may be a new lump, as you really are
> unleavened. For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed. Let us
> therefore celebrate the festival, not with the old leaven, the leaven of
> malice and evil, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth. — 1
> Corinthians 5:7-8 (ESV)

## Understanding the Law and Liberty

The relationship between the believer and the Law (Torah) is a central theme in
the New Testament. Some passages are often cited to argue that the festivals are
no longer relevant, yet a closer examination provides a more nuanced view.

> But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman,
> born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might
> receive adoption as sons. — Galatians 4:4-5 (ESV)

In Galatians 4:8-10, Paul expressed concern for believers who were turning back
to "weak and worthless elementary principles."

> Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature
> are not gods. But now that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by
> God, how can you turn back again to the weak and worthless elementary
> principles of the world, whose slaves you want to be once more? You observe
> days and months and seasons and years! — Galatians 4:8-10 (ESV)

Interpretations of this passage vary. Some view it as a rejection of the
biblical feasts. Others argue that Paul was addressing the Galatians' return to
pagan calendars or an enslavement to the "manner" of observance without the
Spirit of Christ. A similar discussion occurs in the book of Colossians.

> And you, who were dead in your trespasses and the uncircumcision of your
> flesh, God made alive together with him, having forgiven us all our
> trespasses, by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its
> legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross. He disarmed the
> rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in
> him. Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink,
> or with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of
> the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ. — Colossians 2:13-17
> (ESV)

The "record of debt" (Greek: *cheirographon*) refers to a certificate of
indebtedness or a legal proof of sin. In this context, Christ did not
necessarily abolish the Law itself. Instead, He canceled the spiritual debt
incurred by breaking the Law. The festivals, new moons, and Sabbaths are
described as "shadows." These shadows point toward a greater reality found in
Christ.

Observing a feast without Christ is compared to a slave performing a task.
Observing it to honor Christ allows a believer to enter into His presence. This
view suggests that the "substance" or reality is found in Him. The festivals
serve as a means to remember and honor His work. The authority of Jesus over
these sacred times is highlighted by His own words regarding the Sabbath.

> And he said to them, “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath.
> So the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath.” — Mark 2:27-28 (ESV)

Even in the Old Testament, the inclusion of those outside the nation of Israel
was part of the vision for these holy days.

> “And the foreigners who join themselves to the LORD, to minister to him, to
> love the name of the LORD, and to be his servants, everyone who keeps the
> Sabbath and does not profane it, and holds fast my covenant—these I will bring
> to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer; their burnt
> offerings and their sacrifices will be accepted on my altar; for my house
> shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples.” — Isaiah 56:6-7 (ESV)

## The Biblical Commands for the Feast of Weeks

The Feast of Weeks is established in the Old Testament with several names and
specific instructions. It is called the Feast of Harvest, the Feast of Weeks,
and Shavuot in Hebrew. The Greek name, Pentecost, means "fiftieth," referring to
the timing of the event.

> You shall keep the Feast of Harvest, of the firstfruits of your labor, of what
> you sow in the field. — Exodus 23:16 (ESV)
>
> You shall observe the Feast of Weeks, the firstfruits of wheat harvest, and
> the Feast of Ingathering at the year's end. — Exodus 34:22 (ESV)

The book of Deuteronomy emphasizes the voluntary and joyful nature of the
offering.

> Then you shall keep the Feast of Weeks to the LORD your God with the tribute
> of a freewill offering from your hand, which you shall give as the LORD your
> God blesses you. — Deuteronomy 16:10 (ESV)

In Leviticus, the LORD commanded that this festival be observed as a "perpetual
statute" throughout all generations.

> And you shall make a proclamation on the same day. You shall hold a holy
> convocation. You shall not do any ordinary work. It is a statute forever in
> all your dwelling places throughout your generations. — Leviticus 23:21 (ESV)

## Timing of the Festival

The timing of the Feast of Weeks is determined by counting seven full weeks from
the day the sheaf of the firstfruits is brought to the priest.

> You shall count seven full weeks from the day after the Sabbath, from the day
> that you brought the sheaf of the wave offering. You shall count fifty days to
> the day after the seventh Sabbath. Then you shall present a grain offering of
> new grain to the LORD. — Leviticus 23:15-16 (ESV)

This fifty-day period connects the initial harvest of the firstfruits with the
larger wheat harvest. More information on the calculation of this period can be
found on [BibleHub](https://biblehub.com/topical/f/feast_of_weeks.htm).

## Provision for the Poor

A unique aspect of the harvest laws is the requirement to provide for those in
need. This instruction is directly linked to the commandments for the Feast of
Weeks.

> And when you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not reap your field
> right up to its edge, nor shall you gather the gleanings after your harvest.
> You shall leave them for the poor and for the sojourner: I am the LORD your
> God. — Leviticus 23:22 (ESV)

This practice ensured that the blessing of the harvest was shared with the
vulnerable members of society.

## New Testament Fulfillment

The Feast of Weeks reached a significant fulfillment in the New Testament on the
day of Pentecost. Following the resurrection and ascension of Jesus, His
followers were gathered in Jerusalem to observe the festival.

> When the day of Pentecost arrived, they were all together in one place. — Acts
> 2:1 (ESV)

On this day, the Holy Spirit was poured out upon the believers. This event
marked the beginning of a spiritual harvest, where people from many nations
heard the gospel and were added to the church. The physical harvest of the Old
Testament thus serves as a shadow for the spiritual harvest of the New
Testament.
