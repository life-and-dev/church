# The Feast of Weeks: From Harvest to Pentecost

How did an ancient harvest festival become one of the most significant days in
the history of the Christian church? The Feast of Weeks, also known as Shavuot
or Pentecost, bridges the gap between the Old and New Testaments in a profound
way. This article explores the origins of this festival, its biblical
requirements, and its enduring spiritual significance through the lens of
Christ.

## The Relevance of Biblical Feasts Today

There is an ongoing discussion regarding whether the biblical feasts are still
applicable for believers today. Scriptural evidence indicates that the apostles
continued to recognize and observe these days. For instance, the Apostle Paul
expressed a desire to keep the feasts and even hurried his travels to arrive in
Jerusalem in time for Pentecost.

> But on taking leave of them he said, “I will return to you if God wills,” and
> he set sail from Ephesus. — Acts 18:21 (ESV)
>
> For Paul had decided to sail past Ephesus, so that he might not have to spend
> time in Asia, for he was hastening to be at Jerusalem, if possible, on the day
> of Pentecost. — Acts 20:16 (ESV)

Furthermore, the New Testament contains instructions that encourage the
celebration of these festivals in a new spirit. In his first letter to the
Corinthians, Paul connects the sacrifice of Christ to the Passover and the
subsequent festival.

> Cleanse out the old leaven that you may be a new lump, as you really are
> unleavened. For Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed. Let us
> therefore celebrate the festival, not with the old leaven, the leaven of
> malice and evil, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth. — 1
> Corinthians 5:7-8 (ESV)

## From Shadows to Substance in Christ

The transition from the old covenant to the new brought a deeper understanding
of the Law. The Scriptures explain that Christ came at a specific time to redeem
those under the Law.

> But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman,
> born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might
> receive adoption as sons. — Galatians 4:4-5 (ESV)

Paul expressed concern for those who might return to observing religious days in
a legalistic or "enslaved" manner without the freedom found in Christ.

> Formerly, when you did not know God, you were enslaved to those that by nature
> are not gods. But now that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by
> God, how can you turn back again to the weak and worthless elementary
> principles of the world, whose slaves you want to be once more? You observe
> days and months and seasons and years! — Galatians 4:8-10 (ESV)

The core of this understanding is found in the letter to the Colossians. It
explains that the festivals and Sabbaths were "shadows" pointing toward a future
reality.

> Therefore let no one pass judgment on you in questions of food and drink, or
> with regard to a festival or a new moon or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of
> the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ. — Colossians 2:16-17
> (ESV)

In this context, the "ordinances" or the specific legal requirements regarding
how the feasts were performed are seen as fulfilled in Christ. If a person
observes a feast merely as a heavy obligation without the presence of Christ,
they miss the intended blessing. However, when the feasts are observed to honor
Christ, they become an opportunity to enter into the presence of God. This
spiritual perspective prioritizes the relationship with God over rigid adherence
to ceremonies.

### Defense of Feast Observance

Critics often use Colossians 2:16-17 to argue that the feasts are abolished.
However, a different perspective suggests that Paul was not discouraging the
observance itself, but rather warning against being judged by others regarding
the manner of that observance. From this viewpoint, if the "substance" belongs
to Christ, then observing the "shadow" while focused on the substance can
enhance a believer's understanding of God's plan.

## The Biblical Origins of the Feast of Weeks

The Feast of Weeks is known by several names, including Shavuot (the Hebrew word
for "weeks") and Pentecost (the Greek word for "fiftieth"). It was originally
established as a harvest festival.

> You shall keep the Feast of Harvest, of the firstfruits of your labor, of what
> you sow in the field. — Exodus 23:16 (ESV)
>
> You shall observe the Feast of Weeks, the firstfruits of wheat harvest, and
> the Feast of Ingathering at the year's end. — Exodus 34:22 (ESV)

The Israelites were commanded to bring a tribute of a freewill offering as the
LORD had blessed them. This was also a time to remember their history of
deliverance.

> Then you shall keep the Feast of Weeks to the LORD your God with the tribute
> of a freewill offering from your hand, which you shall give as the LORD your
> God blesses you. And you shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt. —
> Deuteronomy 16:10,12 (ESV)

## Divine Requirements and Perpetual Statutes

The timing of the Feast of Weeks was calculated from the day of the offering of
the Firstfruits. The Israelites were instructed to count seven full weeks, or
fifty days.

> You shall count seven full weeks from the day after the Sabbath, from the day
> that you brought the sheaf of the wave offering. You shall count fifty days to
> the day after the seventh Sabbath. Then you shall present a grain offering of
> new grain to the LORD. — Leviticus 23:15-16 (ESV)

The festival included specific commands for the community. These requirements
were described as perpetual statutes for all generations.

- A holy proclamation must be made on that day.
- The community must hold a holy convocation, which is a gathering for ceremony.
- No ordinary or laborious work is permitted.

> And you shall make a proclamation on the same day. You shall hold a holy
> convocation. You shall not do any ordinary work. It is a statute forever in
> all your dwelling places throughout your generations. — Leviticus 23:21 (ESV)

## Provision for the Poor and the Sojourner

An essential aspect of the harvest season was the commandment to care for the
vulnerable. The Law provided specific instructions on how to reap the fields to
ensure that the poor and foreigners were not left without food.

> And when you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not reap your field
> right up to its edge, nor shall you gather the gleanings after your harvest.
> You shall leave them for the poor and for the sojourner: I am the LORD your
> God. — Leviticus 23:22 (ESV)

## The Apostolic Observance of Pentecost

The significance of this feast culminated in the New Testament during the events
recorded in the book of Acts. The disciples were gathered in Jerusalem to
observe the day when the Holy Spirit was poured out.

> When the day of Pentecost arrived, they were all together in one place. — Acts
> 2:1 (ESV)

The Apostle Paul also prioritized this day in his later ministry, demonstrating
its continued importance in the early church.

> But I will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost. — 1 Corinthians 16:8 (ESV)

## Conclusion

The Feast of Weeks serves as a vital link between the agricultural harvests of
ancient Israel and the spiritual harvest of the early church. It began as a
celebration of [firstfruits](#the-biblical-origins-of-the-feast-of-weeks) and
became a [perpetual statute](#divine-requirements-and-perpetual-statutes) that
included [provision for the poor](#provision-for-the-poor-and-the-sojourner).
Ultimately, the feast found its [fullest expression in
Christ](#from-shadows-to-substance-in-christ), as seen in the [apostolic
observance](#the-apostolic-observance-of-pentecost) and the coming of the Holy
Spirit. Through this festival, believers are reminded of God's continued
provision and the spiritual substance found in Christ.
