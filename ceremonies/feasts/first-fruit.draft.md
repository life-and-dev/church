# The Feast of First Fruits (Bikkurim)

The ancient biblical calendar is marked by several appointed times that revolve
around the agricultural seasons of the land of Israel. One of the most
significant yet often overlooked festivals is the Feast of First Fruits. This
observance marks the beginning of the barley harvest and involves a specific set
of ceremonies that emphasize gratitude and anticipation. By examining the
biblical requirements and the historical context of this feast, one can gain a
deeper understanding of its place within the broader religious framework of the
time.

## Biblical Instructions

The primary instructions for the Feast of First Fruits are found in the book of
Leviticus. The festival was to be observed once the Israelites entered the
Promised Land and began to reap its harvest.

> And the LORD spoke to Moses, saying, “Speak to the people of Israel and say
> to them, When you come into the land that I give you and reap its harvest,
> you shall bring the sheaf of the firstfruits of your harvest to the priest,
> and he shall wave the sheaf before the LORD, so that you may be accepted. On
> the day after the Sabbath the priest shall wave it. And on the day when you
> wave the sheaf, you shall offer a male lamb a year old without blemish as a
> burnt offering to the LORD. And the grain offering with it shall be two
> tenths of an ephah of fine flour mixed with oil, a food offering to the LORD
> with a pleasing aroma, and the drink offering with it shall be of wine, a
> fourth of a hin. And you shall eat neither bread nor grain parched or fresh
> until this same day, until you have brought the offering of your God: it is
> a statute forever throughout your generations in all your dwellings.” —
> Leviticus 23:9–14 (ESV)

The "sheaf" (or omer) of the firstfruits of the barley harvest was presented to
the priest. The priest would then wave it before the LORD. This act symbolized
the dedication of the entire harvest to God. The people were prohibited from
eating any of the new grain until this offering was made. This practice
demonstrated a reliance on divine provision and a recognition of God as the
source of the land's abundance (Exodus 23:19, 34:26).

## The Timing Debate

A significant historical and theological debate exists regarding the exact
timing of the Feast of First Fruits. The disagreement centers on the
interpretation of the phrase "on the day after the Sabbath" in Leviticus 23:11.

### The Pharisaic View

The Pharisees interpreted "Sabbath" as a reference to the first day of the
[Feast of Unleavened Bread](unleavened-bread.draft.md). This day is a holy
convocation and a day of rest regardless of which day of the week it falls upon.
According to this view, First Fruits always occurs on the 16th of Nisan. This is
the first month of the Hebrew calendar. This fixed date ensured that the
festival was closely tied to the Passover season.

### The Sadducean View

In contrast, the Sadducees and later the Karaites argued that "Sabbath" refers
to the weekly Sabbath that occurs during the week of Unleavened Bread. Under
this interpretation, the "day after the Sabbath" is always the first day of the
week, or Sunday. This view emphasizes the weekly cycle. It results in a variable
date on the calendar.

### Defense of the Perspectives

Both views seek to remain faithful to the biblical text. The Pharisaic tradition
highlights the sanctity of the festival days themselves as Sabbaths. The
Sadducean tradition maintains a literal interpretation of the weekly rest day.
This debate determines the date for the subsequent [Feast of Weeks](feast-of-weeks.draft.md). The date is calculated by counting fifty days from the Feast of
First Fruits. Detailed arguments for both sides can be found in resources like
[Set Apart People](https://www.setapartpeople.com/when-is-first-fruits-and-how-to-count-the-omer/).

## Messianic Fulfillment

In the New Testament, the Feast of First Fruits is seen as having a profound
fulfillment in the resurrection of Jesus Christ. The timing of the resurrection
aligns with the festival. This is particularly true under the interpretation
that it occurs on the first day of the week.

> But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those
> who have fallen asleep. — 1 Corinthians 15:20 (ESV)

This comparison suggests that just as the firstfruits offering represented the
promise of a greater harvest to follow, the resurrection of Christ serves as the
guarantee of the future resurrection of believers. He is the first to be raised
into an eternal, incorruptible body. This provides hope and assurance for all
who are connected to Him. For more information on the significance of the
resurrection, see the article on [the resurrection](../../bodies.md).

## Conclusion

The Feast of First Fruits emphasizes the importance of giving God the first and
best of one's increase. The [Biblical Instructions](#biblical-instructions)
outline a ceremony of gratitude and dedication. The historical [Timing
Debate](#the-timing-debate) reflects different ways of honoring the biblical
calendar. Ultimately, the [Messianic Fulfillment](#messianic-fulfillment) points
to the hope of eternal life and the promise of a future harvest of souls.
