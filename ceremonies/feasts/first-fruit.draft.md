# Feast of First Fruits

The ancient agricultural cycle of Israel was more than just a means of survival.
It was a rhythmic tapestry of worship and remembrance.
Among the most significant of these moments was the Feast of First Fruits.
This festival marked the beginning of the barley harvest.
It required a unique gesture of gratitude before any of the new crop could be
consumed.
The feast invited the people to acknowledge the providence of the Creator.
What was the purpose of this wave offering?
Why did its timing become a subject of intense debate among religious leaders?

## Biblical Origin and Commandment

The Feast of First Fruits, known in Hebrew as Bikkurim, was established during
the wilderness wanderings.
The LORD commanded the Israelites to observe this festival once they entered the
Promised Land.
It was the third of the spring feasts.
This feast occurred during the week of Unleavened Bread.
The primary requirement was to bring the first sheaf of the barley harvest to
the priest.

> Speak to the people of Israel and say to them, When you come into the land
> that I give you and reap its harvest, you shall bring the sheaf of the
> firstfruits of your harvest to the priest — Leviticus 23:10 (ESV)

The commandment emphasized that the harvest belonged to the LORD.
The people were forbidden from eating any of the new grain until the offering
was made.
This practice instilled a sense of priority and dependence on divine blessing.

> And you shall eat neither bread nor parched grain nor fresh grain until this
> same day, until you have brought the offering of your God: it is a statute
> forever throughout your generations in all your dwellings. — Leviticus 23:14
> (ESV)

## The Ritual of the Wave Sheaf

The central ritual of the day involved the "wave offering" or Tenufah.
A sheaf of barley was brought to the Tabernacle or Temple.
The priest would wave the sheaf before the LORD.
This motion was typically done in four directions.
The act symbolized the presentation of the entire harvest to God.
It served as a petition for acceptance on behalf of the people.
More details on the [Bikkurim ritual](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/first-fruits/)
can be found in historical Jewish records.

> and he shall wave the sheaf before the LORD, so that you may be accepted. On
> the day after the Sabbath the priest shall wave it. — Leviticus 23:11 (ESV)

In addition to the wave sheaf, a burnt offering was required.
The people offered a male lamb without blemish.
This was accompanied by a grain offering mixed with oil and a drink offering of
wine.
These elements represented a complete dedication of the individual's labor and
life to the Creator.

## Timing and the Sabbath Controversy

A significant historical debate exists regarding the exact timing of this feast.
The scripture specifies that the offering occurs on "the day after the Sabbath."
Different Jewish groups interpreted the word "Sabbath" in distinct ways.
The Pharisees believed the Sabbath referred to the first day of the Feast of
Unleavened Bread.
This would place First Fruits on the 16th of Nisan.
The Sadducees and Karaites argued that it referred to the weekly Seventh-day
Sabbath.
In their view, the feast always fell on a Sunday during the week of Unleavened
Bread.
This debate is documented in studies of [ancient sectarian
differences](https://hebrew4christians.com/Holidays/Spring_Holidays/First_Fruits/first_fruits.html).

This distinction is crucial for the counting of the omer.
The omer counting begins on the day of First Fruits.
It leads directly to the [Feast of Weeks](feast-of-weeks.draft.md).
The calculation determines the date of Pentecost.
Most modern Jewish traditions follow the Pharisaic reckoning.
However, many biblical scholars note the alignment of the Sunday observance with
New Testament events.

## Theological Significance and Fulfillment

The New Testament provides a profound interpretation of the Feast of First
Fruits.
Apostle Paul identifies the resurrection of Jesus Christ as the fulfillment of
this shadow.
Jesus rose from the dead on the very day of the wave offering.
He is described as the "firstfruits" of those who have fallen asleep.

> But in fact Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who
> have fallen asleep. — 1 Corinthians 15:20 (ESV)

Just as the first sheaf sanctified the rest of the harvest, the resurrection of
Christ ensures the future resurrection of believers.
This connection transforms the agricultural festival into a celebration of
eternal life.
The "promise to come" inherent in the name Bikkurim finds its ultimate
expression in the empty tomb.

## Defense of Continued Observance

Critiques often argue that the feasts are obsolete for modern believers.
They may cite Colossians 2:16 to suggest these days have no remaining value.
However, a defense of continued observance notes that Paul refers to these days
as a "shadow of things to come."
The substance belongs to Christ.
Observing the feasts can be seen as a way to participate in the "rhythm of the
kingdom."
It provides a structured way to remember the redemptive work of God.
Rather than being a legalistic requirement, it is an educational and devotional
tool.
It connects the believer to the historical and biblical roots of their faith.

## Conclusion

The Feast of First Fruits serves as a powerful reminder of God's provision and
the promise of new life.
It originated as a [barley harvest ritual](#biblical-origin-and-commandment)
that required total dependence on the LORD.
The [wave sheaf ceremony](#the-ritual-of-the-wave-sheaf) symbolized the
dedication of the whole harvest.
While [debates over timing](#timing-and-the-sabbath-controversy) have persisted
for centuries, the
[theological fulfillment](#theological-significance-and-fulfillment)
in the resurrection of Christ remains the central focus for many.
Finally, the [defense of its observance](#defense-of-continued-observance)
highlights its value as a shadow of the greater reality found in Messiah.
