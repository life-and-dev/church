# Church Terminology

This glossary provides definitions for Christology discussions. It distinguishes lexical fact and  contextual inference.

## General Christian Terms

| Term                                                | Short definition                                                                                                                             |
| --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| [Christian](terms/christian.md)                     | A devoted follower of Christ.                                                                                                                |
| [Church](terms/church.md)                           | Originally “the body called to go out” to continue Jesus’s work; now commonly means an assembly of Christians gathered in a church building. |
| [Faith Manifestation](terms/faith-manifestation.md) | Explanations of why great faith produces positive results.                                                                                   |
| [Mystery](terms/mystery.md)                         | A theological term often used to defend illogical doctrines.                                                                                 |
| [Saints](terms/saints.md)                           | Originally referred to all believers, but evolved into an official church title for select individuals.                                      |
| [The Lord's Day](terms/sundays.md)                  | The Christian day of rest that replaced the Sabbath.                                                                                         |
| [Tithing](terms/tithing.md)                         | The obligation to pay the church 10% of income.                                                                                              |

## Biblical Titles and Agency

This table defines this glossary’s principal agency terms. It does not catalogue every biblical title, office, or figure.

> “For there is **one God**, and there is **one mediator** between God and men, the **man Christ Jesus**.” — 1 Timothy 2:5 (ESV)

| Term                                                       | Short definition and evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Angel                                                      | Hebrew *mal’akh* and Greek *angelos* mean “messenger.”                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| [Angel of the LORD](https://son.ofgod.info/son-as-angel)   | The LORD’s messenger. In some narratives the messenger speaks with the sender’s authority. Exod 3:2–6; Judg 6:11–14. **Theological interpretation:** whether particular appearances are the pre-incarnate Son is disputed.                                                                                           |
| Baptism                                                    | Immersion or washing marking repentance, discipleship, or incorporation. Matt 3:6; Acts 2:38; 8:16. Matthew 28:19 names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, but neither defines their metaphysical relation nor records a textual variant that removes the wording.                                                        |
| Beget / Begotten                                           | To father or bring forth; context can include royal enthronement or appointment. Ps 2:7; Acts 13:33; Heb 1:5. **Theological interpretation:** eternal generation is a later Trinitarian account of the Son’s relation to the Father, not the dictionary meaning of the verb.                                         |
| [Firstborn](https://son.ofgod.info/son-as-angel/firstborn) | The firstborn child or, figuratively, the heir with highest rank. Exod 4:22; Ps 89:27; Col 1:15. Context must decide which sense applies.                                                                                                                                                                            |
| Genesis                                                    | Greek *genesis* means origin, birth, or beginning. Matt 1:18; Jas 1:23. It does not by itself decide whether its subject is created, eternally generated, or metaphorically appointed.                                                                                                                               |
| God’s agency                                               | An authorised representative may speak in a sender’s name and carry the sender’s authority. Exod 23:20–21; John 5:43; 13:20. This pattern supports a Unitarian account of representative language, but it is not, by itself, proof that every high claim about Jesus is agency language or that he cannot be divine. |
| Man of God                                                 | A human servant or messenger of God, especially a prophet. Deuternomy 33:1; 1 Samuel 2:27; 1 Kings 17:18.                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| Mediator                                                   | One who stands between parties. The quoted text distinguishes the one God from “the man Christ Jesus”; Trinitarians answer that the incarnate Son mediates in his human role. The verse is evidence in the debate, not a neutral definition of either system.                                                        |
| [Messiah / Christ](https://kingdom.ofgod.info/christ)      | Hebrew *māšîaḥ* and Greek *christos*: “anointed one.” In Israel’s Scriptures, anointing marks kings, priests, and sometimes prophets. [Jesus is God’s promised human Messiah.](https://son.ofgod.info/shema/unitarianism#the-identity-of-jesus).                                                                                               |
| Saviour                                                    | One who rescues or delivers. The LORD is Saviour, and Scripture also applies related deliverance language to appointed agents. Isa 43:11; Judg 3:9; Luke 2:11; Acts 5:31. Calling Jesus Saviour therefore needs context; it does not alone resolve his identity.                                                     |
| Shepherd                                                   | A literal flock keeper and a figurative leader or carer. The LORD and human rulers receive the image. Ps 23:1; Ezek 34:23; John 10:11. Jesus’ claim is significant, but the image alone is not an identity proof.                                                                                                    |
| [Son of God](index.md)                                     | A relational title used for Israel, the Davidic king, angels, believers, and Jesus. Exodus 4:22; Psalm 2:7; Luke 3:38; Rom 8:14. It does not lexically mean “God the Son,” though Trinitarians infer more from particular contexts. See [sons of God](sons-of-god.md#the-sons-of-god).                               |
| [Son of Man](son-of-man.md)                                | Hebrew *ben adam* commonly means a human being; Daniel 7:13 instead uses Aramaic *kəvar enash* (כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ), “like a **son of man**” for the figure receiving authority from the Ancient of Days ([Aramaic text](https://www.mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt3407.htm)).                                                        |

## Divine Language and Attributes

This table defines this glossary’s divine-language and attribute terms. It does not catalogue every attribute, philosophical category, or divine title.

> “To the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the **only God**, be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen.” — 1 Timothy 1:17 (ESV)

| Term                                    | Short definition and evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **God**                                 | Hebrew *el* and *elohim*, and Greek *theos*, can refer to God, gods, or, in limited contexts, exalted representatives; context identifies the referent. Gen 1:1; Exod 7:1; Ps 82:1,6. **Project conclusion:** the New Testament normally identifies the one God as the Father. John 17:3; 1 Cor 8:6. See [one God](https://son.ofgod.info/shema/unitarianism#one-god).                                                                                                                                |
| **YHWH / LORD**                         | YHWH is God’s covenant name in the Hebrew Bible, conventionally rendered “the LORD.” Exod 3:15; 6:2–3; Deut 6:4. “Lord” in the New Testament commonly translates Greek *kyrios* and may refer to God, Jesus, or another authority. An Old Testament quotation or shared title must identify its speaker and context; it is not automatic proof that Jesus is YHWH. See [the Shema](shema.md#what-the-shema-teaches).                                                        |
| **Divine**                              | Of, from, or pertaining to God. Greek *theios* is an adjective, not a personal name. Acts 17:29; 2 Pet 1:3–4. The adjective alone does not establish identity with God. [LSJ, *theios*](https://logeion.uchicago.edu/%CE%B8%CE%B5%E1%BF%96%CE%BF%CF%82) records its lexical range.                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| **Godhead**                             | An older English label in some translations. Romans 1:20 uses *theiotēs* (“divine nature” or quality); Colossians 2:9 uses *theotēs* (“deity” or divine nature). This is a **translation choice**, not a Bible-defined name for three persons. See [translations](godhead.md#translations).                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **One**                                 | Hebrew *echad* and Greek *heis/mia/hen* ordinarily mean one, with sense fixed by grammar and context. Deut 6:4; Mark 12:29; Gal 3:28. **Theological interpretation:** Unitarians read the Shema and Jesus’ citation as identifying the one God as the Father; Trinitarians affirm the same monotheism while construing God’s unity differently. [Brown–Driver–Briggs, *echad*](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/259.htm) does not decide that theological question by etymology. |
| **Monotheism**                          | Belief that only one God exists. Deut 6:4; Isa 45:5; 1 Cor 8:4–6. Both biblical Unitarians and classical Trinitarians claim monotheism; their disagreement concerns Jesus and the Spirit’s relation to the one God.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Omniscience**                         | Knowing all things. Scripture predicates exhaustive knowledge of God. Ps 139:1–6; 1 John 3:20. Jesus’ growth in wisdom and statement about the day or hour are central Unitarian evidence. Luke 2:52; Mark 13:32. Trinitarians relate these to his incarnate human nature; the texts require argument beyond the attribute’s definition.                                                                                                                                    |
| **Omnipresence**                        | Presence everywhere, an attribute ascribed to the LORD. Ps 139:7–10; Jer 23:23–24. A promise of presence can also mean support, representation, or presence through the Spirit; its sense comes from context.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Immortal**                            | Not subject to death. God is described as immortal. Jesus truly died and God raised him. Acts 2:24; Rom 6:9. **Theological interpretation:** Unitarians see this as distinguishing Jesus from God; Trinitarians distinguish the Son’s divine and human natures.                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **Heaven**                              | Sky, celestial realm, or God’s dwelling, according to context. Gen 1:8; Ps 115:3; Matt 6:9. It is not a lexical proof of personal pre-human existence.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Hell / Gehenna**                      | English “hell” covers several terms. *Gehenna* is Jesus’ judgement image, while *Hades* refers to the realm of the dead. Matt 10:28; Luke 16:23. They should not be silently treated as identical.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| **Word**                                | Greek *logos* can mean word, message, account, reason, or matter. John 1:1–14 requires contextual interpretation. **Project conclusion:** John’s Word is God’s self-expression embodied in Jesus, not a second divine person; Trinitarians read the Word as the pre-existent Son. See [Word](word.md#the-meaning-of-the-word).                                                                                                                                              |
| **Wisdom, Light, Life, Way, and Truth** | Scripture can personify qualities and speak of them concretely. Prov 8:1–4; 9:1–3; John 8:12; 14:6. Personification alone does not prove a distinct person, but neither does it settle the identity claims in their Johannine contexts.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |

## Life, Death, and Worship

> “then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a **living creature**.” — Genesis 2:7 (ESV)

| Term                         | Short definition and evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Death**                    | Loss or cessation of life; biblical usage may also describe alienation or a condition requiring resurrection. Gen 3:19; Ezek 18:4; John 5:24. No single afterlife system should be imposed on every occurrence.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Soul**                     | Hebrew *nephesh* and Greek *psychē* can denote a person, self, living creature, life, appetite, or inner life. Genesis 2:7 calls the formed human a living creature. [Brown–Driver–Briggs, *nephesh*](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/5315.htm) and context, rather than an English gloss alone, determine sense.                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **Spirit**                   | Hebrew *ruach* and Greek *pneuma* can mean wind, breath, life, disposition, or spirit. Gen 1:2; Num 11:25; John 3:8; Acts 2:17. **Project conclusion:** the Holy Spirit is God’s Spirit and power, not a separate coequal person. Trinitarians read the Spirit’s personal actions and triadic texts differently. See [Holy Spirit](https://son.ofgod.info/shema/unitarianism#holy-spirit).                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Afterlife**                | Modern umbrella term for life after death. Biblical texts distinguish death, Sheol/Hades, resurrection, judgement, and eternal life. Dan 12:2; John 5:28–29; 1 Cor 15:20–23.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Worship**                  | English umbrella term. Greek *proskyneō* can denote bowing, homage, or worship; *latreuō* commonly denotes religious service. Matt 2:11; 18:26; Luke 4:8; Rom 12:1. Homage to Jesus does not mechanically prove deity, nor does its possible human use decide every occurrence. See [worship](nature.md#worship).                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Pray / Prayer**            | Prayer includes praise, confession, petition, thanksgiving, and address to God. Matt 6:9; Phil 4:6. Jesus prays to God and teaches prayer to the Father. Unitarians take this as a real distinction; Trinitarians do not deny that distinction.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Serve, ordinary / sacred** | *Diakoneō* commonly means serve or minister; *latreuō* commonly concerns religious service. Matt 20:26–28; Luke 4:8; Rom 1:9. English “serve” should not merge these terms without checking context.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Essence / Nature**         | What something is. Classical Christology uses “nature” for divinity and humanity, not for a person or centre of consciousness. Biblical texts do not provide this later technical definition, so its use is a **theological interpretation**, not a lexical gloss. See [nature](nature.md#history-of-christianity).                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Person**                   | In ordinary modern use, an individual self. Classical Trinitarian theology uses “person” for the Father, Son, and Spirit, while affirming one divine essence; it does not mean three separate gods. Christology likewise says the one person of the Son has two natures, divine and human. “Person” and “nature” therefore answer different questions and must not be treated as synonyms. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Trinity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/trinity/) documents the terminology. |

## Language, Names, and Gospel Idioms

**Scope.** These tables list selected language controls relevant to identity, agency, pre-existence, worship, knowledge, death, and Trinity discussion. Greek is the language of the New Testament. A Semitic background should be claimed only where evidence supports it; no Aramaic wording should be invented behind a Greek text.

| Term                          | Short definition and evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Name as authority**         | A name may identify a person, reputation, character, or delegated authority. Exod 23:20–21; John 5:43; Acts 4:12. “In the name of” can express authorised allegiance or authority; it does not itself state shared essence.                                                                                                                                 |
| **Greek *en***                | Usually “in,” with contextual uses including location, sphere, association, and means. John 14:10–11; 17:21–23. The preposition alone does not prove metaphysical mutual indwelling. [LSJ, *en*](https://logeion.uchicago.edu/%E1%BC%90%CE%BD) documents its range.                                                                                         |
| **Ego eimi / “I am”**         | Greek *egō eimi* means “I am” and can be a simple self-identification, with or without an expressed predicate. John 9:9; 8:24,58; 18:5–6. Whether an occurrence alludes to the Greek Old Testament or identifies Jesus with YHWH is a **theological interpretation**, not a translation error. See [John 8:58](word.md#what-about-before-abraham-was-i-am). |
| **See / Know in the Gospels** | These verbs can refer to perception, recognition, experience, or relational knowledge. John 1:18; 14:7–9; 17:3. Speaker, object, and immediate context determine the sense.                                                                                                                                                                                 |

| Expression                    | Language and meaning in context                                                                                             | References                                                                                        |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| **Boanerges**                 | A Semitic form explained as “Sons of Thunder.”                                                                              | Mark 3:17                                                                                         |

| **Amen**                      | Hebrew/Aramaic affirmation, “truly” or “certainly.”                                                                         | Matt 5:18; John 1:51                                                                              |
| **Talitha koum**              | Mark preserves and translates an Aramaic command: “Little girl, I say to you, arise.”                                       | Mark 5:41                                                                                         |
| **Ephphatha**                 | Mark preserves and translates an Aramaic command: “Be opened.”                                                              | Mark 7:34                                                                                         |
| **Korban**                    | A Semitic term for a gift devoted to God.                                                                                   | Mark 7:11                                                                                         |
| **Raca**                      | An Aramaic insult or term of contempt; its exact force is uncertain.                                                        | Matt 5:22                                                                                         |
| **Mammon**                    | Aramaic term for wealth or possessions, personified as a master.                                                            | Matt 6:24; Luke 16:13                                                                             |
| **Messiah (Gospel usage)**    | Hebrew/Aramaic title translated “Christ,” “anointed one.”                                                                   | John 1:41; 4:25                                                                                   |

| **Rabbi / Rabbouni**          | Semitic honorifics meaning teacher / my teacher.                                                                            | John 1:38; 20:16                                                                                  |
| **Hosanna**                   | Hebrew plea, “save, please,” used as an acclamation.                                                                        | Ps 118:25; Matt 21:9                                                                              |
| **Bethesda**                  | Place-name. Its etymology is disputed.                                                                                      | John 5:2                                                                                          |
| **Siloam**                    | John explains the name as “Sent.” Whether this is a full etymology or an interpretative gloss is debated.                   | John 9:7                                                                                          |
| **Gabbatha**                  | John calls it Hebrew and pairs it with Greek “Stone Pavement”; its exact etymology is uncertain.                            | John 19:13                                                                                        |
| **Golgotha**                  | Aramaic place-name explained as “Place of a Skull.”                                                                         | Mark 15:22; John 19:17                                                                            |
| **Eli/Eloi lama sabachthani** | Matthew and Mark preserve related Semitic forms of the opening of Psalm 22:1: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”   | Ps 22:1; Matt 27:46; Mark 15:34                                                                   |
| **Gehenna**                   | Greek form of a Hebrew place-name and judgement image.                                                                      | Jer 7:31–32; Matt 5:22; 10:28                                                                     |

### Semitic Constructions and Narrative Scope

These are reading controls, not hidden translations. A construction is called Semitic only where Hebrew, Aramaic, or Septuagintal influence is well supported. The Gospel wording remains Greek, and a proposed background cannot replace its immediate context.

| Construction                  | Reading control                                                                                                                                               | References                     |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| **Name**                      | Can express identity, reputation, or authority.                                                                                                               | Exod 23:21; John 17:6,11       |
| **Coming from / from heaven** | May express origin, commission, or heavenly source. Context decides whether personal pre-existence is claimed.                                                | John 3:13; 6:38; 16:28         |
| **Before**                    | May express temporal priority, rank, or prior purpose; grammar and context decide.                                                                            | John 8:58; 15:18; 17:5         |
| **Seeing / knowing**          | May concern perception, recognition, or relationship.                                                                                                         | John 6:36; 14:7–9; 17:3        |
| **Hate / love preference**    | “Hate” can express lesser loyalty by comparison, but not in every context.                                                                                    | Gen 29:30–31; Luke 14:26       |
| **Bind / loose**              | Authorise or forbid within community discipline.                                                                                                              | Matt 16:19; 18:18              |
| **Flesh and blood**           | Human limitation or human source.                                                                                                                             | Matt 16:17; Gal 1:16           |
| **Kingdom of heaven**         | Matthew’s reverential circumlocution for God’s kingdom.                                                                                                       | Matt 4:17; 5:3; Mark 1:15      |
| **The Power**                 | Reverential reference to God or the Almighty in the trial sayings.                                                                                            | Matt 26:64; Mark 14:62         |
| **Amen, I say**               | Solemn introductory affirmation.                                                                                                                              | Matt 5:18; John 3:3            |
| **Three days / nights**       | Inclusive Jewish time reckoning may count portions of days, but each passage’s chronology must still be examined.                                             | Esth 4:16; Matt 12:40; 16:21   |
| **This generation**           | Usually Jesus’ contemporaries; context may instead stress a kind of people.                                                                                   | Matt 11:16; 23:36; 24:34       |
| **Evil / good eye**           | Idiom for stinginess or envy, and generosity.                                                                                                                 | Deut 15:9; Matt 6:22–23; 20:15 |
| **Move mountains**            | Hyperbole for overcoming what appears impossible.                                                                                                             | Matt 17:20; 21:21              |
| **Fullness / all / none**     | These Greek quantifiers may be absolute, distributive, or limited by stated scope. This is a contextual observation, not a specifically Semitic construction. | Col 1:19; 2:9; Luke 14:33      |

## Trinity and Creed Vocabulary

This table defines formal vocabulary used in Trinitarian, Unitarian, and historical discussion. These terms arose in post-biblical debates. Their [historical meaning](https://son.ofgod.info/trinity#the-development-of-the-trinity-theology) should be stated before they become conclusions.

| Term                                                         | Short definition and source                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| [Trinity](https://son.ofgod.info/trinity)                    | Classical Christianity’s doctrine that the one God eternally exists as three distinct persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, who share one divine essence. It is not tritheism. [Vatican Catechism §§253–254](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P17.HTM) states this doctrine.                                                                                                                                   |
| **Incarnation**                                              | Classical doctrine that the eternal Son became human in Jesus Christ. In Chalcedonian terms, the one person of the Son exists in two natures, divine and human, “without confusion, change, division, or separation.” This is a doctrinal interpretation of Scripture, not a biblical term. [Council of Chalcedon](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf214.xi.xviii.html) records the definition.                      |
| [Nature](https://son.ofgod.info/nature)                      | In classical Christology, “nature” answers what something is and “person” answers who. Thus “two natures” does not mean two persons, and [“three persons” in the Trinity does not mean three gods](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/trinity/). These technical terms may be challenged, but should not be caricatured.                                                                                                |
| **Trinitas**                                                 | Latin for “triad” or “trinity”; an early Latin theological label, not biblical vocabulary. [Tertullian, *Against Praxeas*](https://www.tertullian.org/latin/adversus_praxean.htm) documents an early use.                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Ousia**                                                    | Greek for being, substance, or essence. Nicene theology uses it for the one divine essence. It is not a Bible-defined three-person formula.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **Hypostasis**                                               | Greek *hypostasis* has context-sensitive New Testament senses, including confidence, reality, and underlying reality. Heb 1:3; 3:14; 11:1. Later pro-Nicene theology uses it for a distinct person or subsistence; that later use should not be read uncritically into every New Testament occurrence.                                                                                                                  |
| **Homoousios**                                               | Greek for “of the same essence/substance”; the Nicene description of the Son’s relation to the Father. It is post-biblical creed vocabulary. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Trinity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/trinity/) discusses the term.                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Homoiousios**                                              | Greek for “of similar essence/substance”; a fourth-century alternative in the controversy over the Son. [Britannica: Arianism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arianism) documents the dispute.                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Substantia / essentia**                                    | Latin: substance / essence; Latin renderings used for what God is. They are later metaphysical terms, not Bible definitions. [Augustine, *De Trinitate*, Latin text](https://www.augustinus.it/latino/trinita/trinita_05_libro.htm) uses this Latin vocabulary.                                                                                                                                                         |
| Person                                                       | An individual subject able to understand, intend, and act. Mind and will are faculties of a person.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| [Persona](https://alatius.com/ls/index.php?l=persona)        | A Latin term with historical ranges including mask, role, and legal person. Later Western Trinitarian theology uses it as a technical term. That technical use differs from the modern English sense of “person” and should not be projected unchanged onto biblical language. See [New Advent: Person](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/11726a.htm).                                                                   |
| **Consubstantial**                                           | Sharing one substance or essence; a common translation of *homoousios*.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Coequal / Coeternal**                                      | Equal in divine status / eternal together without beginning. These are creed conclusions, not biblical definitions. [Athanasian Creed](https://www.crcna.org/welcome/beliefs/creeds/athanasian-creed) uses this language.                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Perichoresis / circumincession**                           | Later terms for the mutual indwelling of the three divine persons. They are not biblical definitions. [Merriam-Webster: *perichoresis*](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/perichoresis) records the theological use.                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| **Filioque**                                                 | Latin “and from the Son,” the Western addition concerning the Spirit’s procession. It is historical creed language, not biblical vocabulary.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Eternal generation**                                       | Doctrine that the Son is eternally begotten of the Father, without a temporal beginning. It is a Trinitarian interpretation of biblical sonship language, not its lexical definition.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Procession**                                               | In Trinitarian doctrine, the eternal origin of the Spirit from the Father, or from the Father and Son in Western usage.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Communicatio idiomatum**                                   | Latin for “communication of properties”; the Christological rule that attributes of either nature may be predicated of Christ’s one person. This explains why Trinitarians can affirm both Jesus’ real death and the Son’s divine immortality without saying that the divine nature died. [Book of Concord, Solid Declaration VIII](https://bookofconcord.org/solid-declaration/person-of-christ/) states the doctrine. |
| [Triad](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/triad)         | A group of three entities, not necessary gods.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| Tritheism                                                    | Belief in [three gods](https://iep.utm.edu/trinity/) rather than one God.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| [Biblical Unitarianism](https://son.ofgod.info/unitarianism) | Here, biblical Unitarian Christianity: one God, the Father; Jesus is God’s Messiah; the Holy Spirit is God’s Spirit. Unitarians are Christians, though they reject the classical Trinity.                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| [Modalism](https://son.ofgod.info/trinity#modalism)          | View that the one God appears in different modes rather than existing as distinct Father, Son, and Spirit. It differs from both classical Trinitarianism and biblical Unitarianism.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Subordinationism                                             | A family of views that regards the Son, and sometimes the Spirit, as divine but subordinate to the Father. The Son may be subordinate in role and authority, or as a lesser divine being in nature or status.                                                                                                                                                                                                           |

| [Arianism](https://son.ofgod.info/trinity#arianism)          | A fourth-century family of views in which the Son is created or unlike the Father in a disputed sense.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
