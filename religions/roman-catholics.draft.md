# Simon Magus (the Pater)

The identity of the first Bishop of Rome is a cornerstone of ecclesiastical
history and theological authority. While tradition often points to the Apostle
Peter, historical records and biblical accounts suggest a more complex narrative
involving Simon Magus. This article explores the origins of Simon Magus, his
influence in Samaria, and the historical debates surrounding the leadership of
the early church in Rome.

## Simon Magus in Samaria

One of Paul's earliest epistles, estimated to be written around 50 AD, already
warned about the coming apostasy. He noted that the mystery of lawlessness was
already at work during his ministry.

> For the mystery of lawlessness **is already at work**. — 2 Thessalonians 2:7 (ESV)

Luke did not record every event of the early church in the book of Acts.
However, it was important enough for him to record the account of Simon Magus,
also known as the Pater, as this man started a counterfeit form of Christianity.
Several key details are known about Simon Magus:

* Simon was a Samaritan rather than a Jew. This is significant because the Bible states that salvation was of the Jews and not of the Samaritans. — John 4:22 (ESV)
* He used demonic powers to perform miracles and wonders. — Acts 8:9 (ESV)
* The entire population of Samaria, both small and great, gave heed to him and worshipped him as a god. — Acts 8:10 (ESV)
* Simon established himself as "the Great One." — Acts 8:9,11 (ESV)
* He was originally a believer and was even baptized. — Acts 8:13 (ESV)
* Simon recognized that the power of Christ was greater than his own. — Acts 8:13 (ESV)
* He saw the potential in the early Christian movement and tried to bribe Peter and John to obtain apostleship. — Acts 8:18-21 (ESV)
* Although Simon believed and was baptized, he later refused to repent of his wicked intentions. — Acts 8:22 (ESV)
* Peter prophesied that Simon would become like bitter gall and a bond of lawlessness. — Acts 8:23 (ESV)
* Jude also issued warnings about people who shared the characteristics of Simon. — Jude 4 (ESV)
* The Samaritans were known to worship pagan deities under the disguise of fearing the LORD. — 2 Kings 17:24-41 (ESV)
* The word "Magus" is derived from a Chaldean or Persian term for a priest.

## Historical Perspectives on Simon Magus

Church historians have noted the syncretic nature of the system established by
Simon Magus. He blended various traditions to create a universal religious
system.

> Harnack, a church historian, states that Simon Magus "proclaimed a doctrine in which the Jewish faith was strangely and grotesquely mixed with BABYLONIAN myths, together with some Greek additions. The mysterious worship . . . in consequence of the widened horizon and the deepening religious feeling, finally the wild SYNCRETISM [that is, blending together of religious beliefs], whose aim WAS A UNIVERSAL RELIGION, all contributed to gain adherents for Simon" — Adolph Harnack, [History of Dogma, Vol. 1, p. 244](https://archangel16.livejournal.com/180135.html)

> Simon — the “Pater,” or father, of this counterfeit religion — did not accomplish his goal in his lifetime. But succeeding leaders, with the church’s headquarters moved to Rome, did later gain political control over the Roman Empire and its medieval successor, the “Holy Roman Empire.” — [The Trumpet](https://www.thetrumpet.com/10217-do-you-know-the-truth-about-the-trinity)

## Claims Regarding the Roman Bishopric

Traditional accounts of church history often place the Apostle Peter in Rome.
However, some researchers suggest that the historical "Simon" in Rome may have
been Simon Magus rather than Simon Peter.

> The teaching of Catholic historians tells us that Simon Peter went to Rome at the same time as Simon Magus in order to thwart his evils. This was during the reign of Claudius. After successfully combating the Magus, they tell us, Peter assumed the Roman bishopric and ruled it until the Neronian persecutions of 68 A.D., during which Peter was supposed to have been crucified upside down on Vatican hill. This is the basic story and Catholic writers never shirk in attempting to defend it. Some of them say that this general account is one of the most provable of historical events. But is it? — [Points of Truth Ministries](http://www.pointsoftruth.com/articles/SimonMagus.html)

> The fact remains, many ecclesiastical authors of the second century, Justin Martyr among them, give information completely negating Peter’s supposed Roman bishopric. This is admitted by virtually all scholars – except conservative Catholics (Ency. Biblica, col. 4554). But, more important than this, the records of the True Church of God — the writings of the New Testament — absolutely refute the Roman Catholic claim. — [Points of Truth Ministries](http://www.pointsoftruth.com/articles/SimonMagus.html)

The claim that the Apostle Peter served as the Bishop of Rome remains a central
point of contention in church history. While tradition is strong, the absence of
early second-century evidence for Peter’s presence in Rome provides a basis for
critical examination.

Critiques of this view often point to the long-standing tradition of the church
and the archaeological sites associated with Peter in Rome. However, defenders
of the alternative view argue that these traditions developed later. They
emphasize that the earliest writers, such as Justin Martyr, do not mention
Peter's presence in Rome while discussing Simon Magus. This suggests that the
identification of Peter with the Roman bishopric may have been a later
historical development.
