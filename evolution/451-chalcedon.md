# 451 (Council of Chalcedon)

The Council of Chalcedon met in 451 to settle a question that had divided Christian leaders: 

*How could Jesus Christ be spoken of as **both truly human and truly divine** without making him **2** separate subjects?*

## Why the Council Met

Earlier councils had already called Jesus the Son of God and spoke of him as ***[divine and human](https://son.ofgod.info/nature)***. The dispute was how those claims fitted together. Church leaders wanted to **protect two convictions at once**:

1. Jesus was **one Christ**, not two separate sons or agents.
2. The **humanity of Jesus was real**, not lost or changed by union with divinity.

Nestorius became a focus of the first concern. He rejected the idea of two Sons, although his critics feared that his way of distinguishing divinity and humanity divided Christ into two subjects. [Britannica's account of Nestorius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nestorius) notes that it is doubtful he taught the later position called Nestorianism. The Council of Ephesus condemned him in 431, but the underlying question remained.

Eutyches became a focus of the other concern. He was reported to say that Christ was *“of two natures”* before the union but one after it. His opponents feared that this language made Jesus' **humanity no longer fully distinct**. [Britannica's account of Eutyches](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Eutyches) records this dispute.

Chalcedon therefore met to reverse the 449 settlement and state a formula that rejected both feared outcomes. The council sought one acting Son while retaining complete divinity and complete humanity. Its [Acts and Definition](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3811.htm) preserve the council's decision in an old English translation.

## What Chalcedon Decided

The Definition says Jesus Christ is “one and the same Son” in ***two*** complete natures, ***divine and human***.

In simple terms, *person* answers “who is this?” The council's answer was one Son.

*Nature* answers “what is he?” Its answer was fully divine and fully human.

The council did not teach two Sons, two Christs, or a divine being merely wearing a human body.

It described this union with four safeguards: “without confusion, without change, without division, without separation”. The first two deny that divinity and humanity mixed into a third thing or that either ceased to be what it was. The last two deny that the natures made two persons or could be split apart.

The Definition also says **Christ has a rational human soul and body**. These phrases are [the council's teaching](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/chalcedon.asp), not wording taken directly from Scripture.

The council did not invent [the Trinity](https://son.ofgod.info/trinity) or the claim that Christ was divine and human. It supplied *a rule for holding those claims together*.

## How It Influenced Christianity

Chalcedon made its formula a test of accepted teaching in churches that received the council. Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches, and later most Protestant traditions, **inherited the formula of one Son with two complete natures**. It gave their teachers and creeds a shared way to explain why the Gospels show Jesus hungry, suffering, praying, and dying, while other texts are read as speaking of *divine glory* or *pre-existence*. In this framework, human statements are said of the Son according to his human nature, and divine statements according to his divine nature.

The decision also contributed to a lasting **divide among churches**. Many churches now called [Oriental Orthodox](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oriental-Orthodoxy), including Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, and Ethiopian churches, rejected Chalcedon's definition and authority.

The [Oriental Orthodox](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Oriental-Orthodoxy) and Chalcedonians both affirm that the one Christ is *fully divine and fully human*.

The difference becomes clear in one claim: **“After the incarnation, Christ is in two natures.”** Chalcedonians affirm it.

* Chalcedonians say his [divine and human natures remain distinct](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3811.htm), though both belong to one person.
* Miaphysites traditionally resist that wording because counting two natures after their union can suggest **two separately existing Christs**. They instead call the incarnate Christ *one united divine-human nature*.

Chalcedonians likewise deny that Christ is two persons ([1989 agreed statement](https://orthodoxjointcommission.wordpress.com/2013/12/14/first-agreed-statement-1989/)). The dispute concerns *how to describe the united Christ*.

The formula thus shaped later creeds, how many Christians read biblical passages about Jesus, and which churches recognise one another's teaching as orthodox.

## Scripture's Challenge to Two Natures

Merely showing that [the Father and Son are distinct](https://son.ofgod.info/son-as-man/distinct) does not refute neither Chalcedon nor Miaphysite claims. They distinguishes them too. The question is **whether Scripture identifies** Jesus as possessing a divine nature in addition to being the human Messiah.

Peter describes Jesus as “a **man attested to you by God**” through works “that **God did through him**” (Acts 2:22). He then declares that “**God has made him both Lord and Christ**” (Acts 2:36). Paul calls the mediator between God and humanity “the **man Christ Jesus**” (1 Timothy 2:5). The passages attribute Jesus’ works and **appointed authority from God**, instead of a second divine nature.

**Jesus' is still dependant on God after his earthly life.** Paul says that, after the kingdom’s enemies are subdued, “the **Son himself** will also be subjected” to God (1 Corinthians 15:28). This makes the Son’s relation to God part of the final scene, not merely a temporary limit before his resurrection.

## Conclusion

Chalcedon defined [one Son in two complete natures](#what-chalcedon-decided), seeking to avoid both a divided Christ and an incomplete humanity.

Its formula became a major Christian standard for Chalcedonian traditions and helped form the lasting [division from Oriental Orthodox churches](#how-it-influenced-christianity).
