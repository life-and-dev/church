# The History of the Church

The history of the Christian church is a complex tapestry of faith, politics,
and cultural shifts. From its humble beginnings as a Jewish movement to becoming
a dominant global institution, the journey of the church has been marked by
profound changes. How did the early teachings of Jesus evolve into the
structured dogmas of later centuries? This article explores the key influencers
and events that shaped the church's identity and practices over the millennia.

## Jewish Influences

The foundations of the Christian faith were deeply rooted in Jewish tradition
and the socio-political climate of the era.

- **Before the Messiah**: During the [Jewish
  Era](evolution/-1445-jewish-era.md), a significant shift occurred in how the
  divine name was handled. The Tetragrammaton, the sacred name of God, was
  increasingly replaced with titles like "Adonai" or "Kyrios" in public reading
  and translations. This practice aimed to prevent the misuse of the holy name.
  It also changed the personal nature of divine address. [Learn more about the
  Tetragrammaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetragrammaton).
- **At the Messiah**: The arrival of [Jesus](evolution/1st-c-jesus.md) brought
  about the fulfillment of messianic prophecies, revolutionary teachings, and
  the pivotal event of the crucifixion. These events redefined the relationship
  between God and humanity. They set the stage for a new spiritual movement.
- **After the Messiah**: In the [Messianic Era](evolution/1st-c-messianic.md),
  the message spread rapidly despite intense persecution. This period was
  characterized by the initial dispersion of believers and the systematic
  sharing of the Gospel across the Roman world.

## Christian Influences

As the movement expanded, it encountered various philosophical and political
forces that significantly altered its course.

- **Early Development**: In the 1st century, the followers of Jesus officially
  adopted the name "[Christian](evolution/1st-c-christians.md)." Simultaneously,
  the [Roman Imperial Cult](evolution/1st-c-roman-imperial-cult.md) intensified
  persecution. It also altered the calendar to honor Greek and Roman gods.
  [Gnostic](evolution/1st-c-gnosis.md) wisdom also began to influence
  Christianity through various speculative interpretations.
- **Institutionalization and Dogma**: By 156 AD,
  [Montanism](evolution/156-montanism.md) encouraged more authoritative church
  roles and the canonization of the Bible. In 206 AD,
  [Tertullian](evolution/206-tertullian.md) introduced foundational concepts
  like the Trinity, apostolic succession, and the Eucharist. The [Crisis of the
  Third Century](evolution/235-crisis-of-3rd-century.md) saw the fall of
  "divine" emperors, which shifted the political landscape.
- **The Imperial Church**: Under [Emperor
  Constantine](evolution/312-constantine.md) in 312 AD, the Roman Catholic
  Church was institutionalized. During this period, the sun god, Sol Invictus,
  was often considered "Jesus Christ." [The first Nicaea
  Creed](evolution/325-nicaea-creed.md) in 325 AD further supported church
  authority through the government. Christians were obliged to confess Jesus'
  divinity. Jewish feasts were replaced with "Christian" feasts. Christianity
  became mixed with paganism. [Sol Invictus and
  Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sol_Invictus#Sol_Invictus_and_Christianity).
- **Legalization and State Power**: Subsequent rulers and councils continued
  this trajectory. In 337 AD, Constantius II allowed the [spread of
  Arianism](evolution/337-arianism.md) in Gothic regions. In 363 AD, the Council
  of Laodicea [instituted Sunday rest](evolution/363-outlawing-sabbath.md) and
  persecuted those who kept the Sabbath. [Canon 29 of the Council of
  Laodicea](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3806.htm) specifically prohibited
  resting on the Sabbath. By 379 AD, [Nicene Christianity became a state
  religion](evolution/380-state-religion.md) under Theodosius I. Other
  "heresies" were criminalized. [St. Jerome's Vulgate
  Bible](evolution/382-vulgate.md) in 382 AD provided the first Latin version
  aligned with Nicene orthodoxy.
- **The Middle Ages**: The [Dark Ages](evolution/395-dark-ages.md) saw the fall
  of Arian nations while Nicene Christianity survived. Popes began instituting
  new holidays like [Valentine's Day](evolution/494-valentine.md) and [All
  Saint's Day](evolution/609-halloween.md). The [Holy Roman
  Empire](evolution/800-holy-roman-empire.md) strengthened papal involvement in
  politics. Papal influence spread across Europe. Any religious practices
  against the Roman Catholic Church were suppressed.
- **Medieval Restrictions**: In 1074 AD, Pope Gregory VII [prohibited clerical
  marriages](evolution/1074-clerical-marriages.md). The
  [Crusades](evolution/1095-crusades.md) began in 1095 AD as soldiers believed
  salvation could be secured through these wars. In 1215 AD, Pope Innocent III
  decreed [seven sacraments](evolution/1215-7-sacraments.md) necessary for
  salvation. The [Council of Toulouse](evolution/1229-bible-ownership.md) in
  1229 prohibited laypeople from owning Bibles. Finally, in 1302 AD, Pope
  Boniface VIII declared that [submission to the Pope is necessary for
  salvation](evolution/papal-authority.drafts.md). [Read the Bull Unam
  Sanctam](https://www.papalencyclicals.net/bon08/b8unam.htm).

## Defense of the Historical Narrative

Critiques of this historical narrative often point to the concept of organic
development rather than sudden invention. Proponents of the Trinity argue that
while the terminology was formalized by figures like Tertullian, the underlying
belief was present from the beginning of the church. They might cite Matthew
28:19 (ESV) as early evidence of a triune formula. Similarly, the
institutionalization under Constantine is viewed by some as a necessary step for
the church's survival and growth. However, proponents of the "falling away"
theory argue that these changes represented a significant departure from
original apostolic teachings. They often point to the warnings of Paul regarding
a coming apostasy in 2 Thessalonians 2:3 (ESV).

## Conclusion

The history of the church reveals a journey from a Jewish messianic movement to
a powerful global institution. Key moments such as the [institutionalization
under Constantine](#christian-influences) and the [shift in sacramental
theology](#christian-influences) highlight the significant transformations in
belief and practice. By understanding these [Jewish](#jewish-influences) and
[Christian](#christian-influences) influences, one can better appreciate the
complex evolution of modern theological traditions. A deeper look at the
[historical narrative](#defense-of-the-historical-narrative) provides a balanced
perspective on these developments.
